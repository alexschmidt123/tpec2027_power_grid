\begin{abstract}
Active probing can reduce uncertainty in power-system dynamics by collecting informative measurements about unknown inertia and frequency-response parameters. In sequential probing, each pulse affects both the current measurement and the physical state carried into later experiments. This paper compares three adaptive policy-based methods---deep adaptive design (DAD), reinforcement-learning-based sequential Bayesian optimal experimental design (RL-sBOED), and Step-DAD---with Random, Fixed, and Myopic baselines on reduced IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus systems. Across three to five probes, the adaptive policy-based methods achieve higher mean sequential prior-contrastive information scores than the baselines. On IEEE 9-bus, DAD improves over Fixed by 0.09--0.13 nats while requiring only 1.01--1.75 ms per sequence for online decisions on the profiled hardware. Step-DAD provides small additional gains on IEEE 9-bus but at substantially higher online cost, with no consistent improvement on IEEE 14-bus. Overall, the results show that adaptive policy-based probing can improve information acquisition while reducing the online computation required for sequential experiment design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Reliable power-system operation depends on knowing how the grid responds to disturbances. Inertia (M) and frequency response (K) are important for short-term frequency dynamics, and their effective values can change as inverter-based resources alter the generation mix~\cite{denholm2020inertia}. Disturbance-based methods estimate inertia from measured frequency and active-power responses~\cite{wall2014inertia}. Active probing takes a different approach: a small test signal is injected into the system and the resulting response is measured. The design of that signal affects the information obtained from the experiment. This has been shown for electromechanical-mode identification~\cite{pierre2010probing} and, more recently, for inertia and frequency-response estimation in systems with inverter-based resources~\cite{peng2024probing}.

This paper considers a sequence of probing pulses. The pulse amplitude and application bus are fixed, and the duration is selected at each stage. The system is not reset between probes. As a result, each duration changes the current response and also the physical state carried into the next probing window. The value of a duration therefore depends on the measurements and decisions that came before it. This makes probe selection a sequential design problem, while repeated online search can add substantial computation to the experiment. The setting is related to excitation design in system identification~\cite{hjalmarsson2009identification}, but here the design is updated over a carried-state probing sequence.

Bayesian optimal experimental design (BOED) provides a way to choose probes by maximizing expected information gain about the unknown parameters~\cite{huan2016sequential}. A Fixed design can optimize the full sequence before probing begins, but it cannot use measurements collected during the experiment. A Myopic design can react to those measurements, but it optimizes only the next probe and repeats the search online. We refer to DAD~\cite{foster2021dad}, RL-sBOED~\cite{blau2022rlsboed}, and Step-DAD~\cite{hedman2025stepdad} collectively as \emph{adaptive policy-based methods}. DAD and RL-sBOED learn history-dependent policies offline, whereas Step-DAD starts from a trained DAD policy and further refines it online as measurements become available.

We evaluate these adaptive policy-based methods for continuous pulse-duration selection on reduced IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus systems. Random, Fixed, and Myopic designs are used as baselines. All six methods use the same physical model, endpoint RoCoF sensor, duration constraints, and probe budget. The comparison focuses on two questions: how much information is acquired over the probing sequence and how much computation is required to choose the probes. We also rescore the IEEE 14-bus histories with larger contrast sets to examine sensitivity to the information estimator. Code and archived results are available online.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/alexschmidt123/tpec2027_power_grid}}

\subsection{Uncertain Parameters and Reduced Swing Dynamics}
For a reduced network with $m$ retained machines, the unknown parameter vector is $\theta=(M_1,\ldots,M_m,K_1,\ldots,K_m)$. Here $M_i$ is the inertia coefficient and $K_i$ is an effective frequency-response coefficient for machine $i$. We assign a joint prior $p_0(\theta)$ constituting independent uniform priors, $M_i\sim\mathcal U(0.7\bar M_i,1.3\bar M_i)$ and $K_i\sim\mathcal U(0.1\bar K_i,\bar K_i)$. \hl{what are $\bar M_i, \bar K_i$?} One parameter vector is drawn at the start of a probing sequence and remains fixed throughout that sequence.

Rotor angles $\delta_i$ and angular-speed deviations $\omega_i$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot\delta_i &= \omega_i,\\
M_i\dot\omega_i
 &= u_i-\left(D_i+\frac{K_i}{2\pi}\right)\omega_i-\sum_{j=1}^{m}B_{ij}\sin(\delta_i-\delta_j),
\end{aligned}\label{eq:swing}
\end{equation}
where $u_i$ is the probing input, $D_i$ is the known damping coefficient, and $B_{ij}$ is the network coupling. \hl{possible not use $D_i$ here and in Table 1 as you used $D_t$ in equatin 2?} Dots denote derivatives with respect to $d$, and the frequency deviation is $\Delta f_i=\omega_i/(2\pi)$ Hz. The coefficient $K_i$ represents an effective power response to frequency deviation; turbine--governor dynamics are not modeled separately. We use a 100-MVA, 60-Hz base. The initial rotor angles, speed deviations, and net mechanical injections are zero, so each sequence starts from an unforced equilibrium.

The reduced IEEE 9-bus model retains buses $(1,2,3)$, and the IEEE 14-bus model retains buses $(1,2,3,6,8)$. A lossless network Laplacian $\mathbf L$ is formed from the MATPOWER test-case reactances~\cite{zimmerman2011matpower}. Algebraic buses are removed by Kron reduction, $\mathbf L_{\mathrm{red}}=\mathbf L_{gg}-\mathbf L_{gl}\mathbf L_{ll}^{-1}\mathbf L_{lg}$, with $B_{ij}=-(\mathbf L_{\mathrm{red}})_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$. Resistance, voltage dynamics, and transformer tap effects are omitted.

Nominal inertia is computed as $\bar M_i=2H_i/(2\pi f_0)$ with $f_0=60$ Hz. For IEEE 9-bus, $H=(23.64,6.40,3.01)$ s is taken from published machine data~\cite[Table~2]{kaur2016transient}. For IEEE 14-bus, we use $H=(4,6.5,5,5,5)$ s as a benchmark setting. The nominal response coefficient is $\bar K_i=K_{\mathrm{tot}}w_i$, where $K_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.5$ pu/Hz. The weights are $w=(0.5875,0.2625,0.15)$ for IEEE 9-bus and $w=(8,13,10,10,10)/51$ for IEEE 14-bus. Damping is set to $D_i=(0.1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1})\bar M_i$. Table~\ref{tab:physical} lists the resulting values. The IEEE 14-bus inertia, response, damping, and prior ranges are benchmark assumptions and should not be interpreted as validated operating parameters.

\subsection{Pulse Design, State Carry, and Sensing}
Each probe is described by $\xi_t=(A_t,B_t,D_t)$, where $A_t$ is the amplitude, $B_t$ is the application bus, and $D_t$ is the duration. We fix $A_t=0.05$ pu and $B_t=1$ and optimize only $D_t$. The probe has a raised-cosine shape,
\begin{equation}
u_{B_t,t}(d)=\frac{A_t}{2}\bigl[1-\cos(2\pi d/D_t)\bigr],
\quad 0\le d\le D_t,\label{eq:probe}
\end{equation}
and is zero after $D_t$ and at all other buses. Every stage uses a fixed $D_{\mathrm{obs}}=3.5$s recording window. The terminal rotor angles and speed deviations are carried directly into the next stage. Thus, the response at stage $t$ depends on the durations applied earlier in the same sequence.

The noise-free scalar observation is the signed endpoint rate of change of frequency (RoCoF) at bus 1:
\begin{equation}
y_t(\theta,\xi_{1:t})=
\frac{\Delta f_{1,t}(D_{\mathrm{obs}})-\Delta f_{1,t}(D_{\mathrm{obs}}-\Delta d_{\mathrm{samp}})}{\Delta d_{\mathrm{samp}}},
\label{eq:sensor}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta d_{\mathrm{samp}}=0.025$s. \hl{what are $\xi$, $\Delta f$ here? how this connect to the system equation (1)? } Measurement noise is modeled as
\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{obs},t}=y_t(\theta,\xi_{1:t})+\epsilon_t,
\qquad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),\label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
with independent Gaussian noise and $\sigma=0.005$Hz/s. The sensor therefore reports signed RoCoF at the end of the recording window, not the initial inertial RoCoF or the maximum absolute RoCoF during the stage. The next duration is selected after this measurement is available. Decision latency is not modeled in the physical simulation.

Durations satisfy the common constraints
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
0.2&\le D_t\le3,&&t=1,\ldots,T,\\
D_t-D_{t-1}&\ge\Delta D_{\min},&&t=2,\ldots,T,
\end{aligned}\label{eq:bounds}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta D_{\min}=0.01$s. At each stage, the feasible interval is shortened as needed so that the remaining probes can still satisfy the increasing-duration constraint. The same continuous action mapping is used for all methods. This ordering rule is a benchmark restriction rather than a requirement of the swing equations. Because pulse energy is proportional to $A_tD_t/2$, equal probe counts do not imply equal injected energy.

\subsection{History-Dependent Decisions and Bayesian Updating}
After stage $t$, the observed history is $h_t=((\xi_k,y_{\mathrm{obs},k}))_{k=1}^t$, with $h_0=\varnothing$. A policy $\pi\in\Pi$ selects the next probe from this history only; it does not observe the true parameter vector or the true internal state. For any candidate $\theta$, the simulator predicts the response to the applied duration sequence and defines the Gaussian likelihood in~\eqref{eq:noise}. Bayes' rule gives
\begin{equation}
p(\theta\mid h_t)=
\frac{p(y_{\mathrm{obs},t}\mid\theta,h_{t-1},\xi_t)\,p(\theta\mid h_{t-1})}
{p(y_{\mathrm{obs},t}\mid h_{t-1},\xi_t)}.\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
with $p(\theta\mid h_0)=p_0(\theta)$. Each candidate parameter vector carries its own predicted state through the observed duration sequence. Since the applied action is already determined by the observed history, the posterior update does not require a policy-action probability term.

\subsection{Full-Sequence Information Objective}
Expected information gain (EIG) is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I_T(\pi)=\E_{h_T\mid\pi}
\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(\theta\mid h_T)\,\|\,p_0(\theta)\bigr)\right],
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is the Kullback--Leibler divergence. The expectation is taken over parameter draws, measurement noise, and any policy randomness. We seek a policy that solves
\begin{equation}
\pi^\star\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal I_T(\pi).
\label{eq:design_problem}
\end{equation}
Information is measured in nats. Fixed and the three adaptive policy-based methods are evaluated with the full-sequence objective. Myopic approximately maximizes one-step conditional information gain, while Random does not optimize an information objective.

Direct EIG evaluation requires integrating the nonlinear likelihood over the prior. We instead use the sequential prior-contrastive (sPCE) lower bound~\cite{foster2021dad}. One prior draw, $\theta^{(0)}$, generates the observed sequence, and $L$ additional prior draws serve as contrasts. For a candidate parameter vector, the likelihood of the observed history is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_t(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{t}
p(y_{\mathrm{obs},k}\mid\theta,h_{k-1},\xi_k).
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
The sampled score is
\begin{equation}
\widehat I_t=\log\frac{\mathcal L_t(\theta^{(0)})}
{\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\mathcal L_t(\theta^{(\ell)})}.
\label{eq:spce}
\end{equation}
Likelihoods are evaluated in the log domain. The mean terminal score estimates the sPCE lower bound rather than EIG itself. A sampled score can be negative and is bounded above by $\log(L+1)$; for $L=128$, the ceiling is 4.8598 nats. We therefore call the reported values \emph{information scores} and later check their sensitivity to larger contrast sets. The generating parameters are used by the simulator and scorer only and are never given to a design method.

\section{Design Methods}\label{sec:methods}
All six methods use the same simulator, observation model, and duration constraints. The main differences are whether measurements are used to adapt later probes, whether the objective covers one step or the full sequence, and whether optimization is performed offline or during probing.

\subsection{Nonadaptive Baselines: Random and Fixed}
Random samples one unit action at each stage and maps it to the remaining feasible duration interval. It uses no measurement feedback and requires no offline training. Because the feasible interval changes after each decision, the resulting complete sequences are not uniformly distributed.

Fixed optimizes the full duration sequence before test measurements are observed, following the nonadaptive formulation in~\cite[Sec.~2.4]{huan2016sequential}. It uses terminal sPCE as the objective but does not adapt during probing. Several interior initializations are compared first, after which the best initialization is optimized with pathwise gradients. A separate Fixed sequence is obtained for each training seed.

\subsection{Online One-Step Baseline: Myopic}
Myopic uses the current history to choose the next duration by approximately maximizing one-step conditional information gain~\cite[Sec.~2.4]{huan2016sequential}. The posterior is represented by 128 weighted particles, each carrying its own physical state. At every decision, three rounds of 16 candidate durations are searched, using 32 simulated observations per candidate. No offline training is required, but posterior updating and candidate search are repeated during probing. The selected action is therefore an approximate myopic optimum.

\subsection{Adaptive Policy-Based Methods}
The adaptive policy-based methods use the observed history to select later probes. Their policy input contains the stage index, previous durations, scaled observations, and masks for unused history slots. Past probes remain ordered because the physical state is carried across stages. DAD and RL-sBOED learn the policy offline and select a duration at test time with a policy forward pass followed by the common feasible-duration mapping. Step-DAD uses the same history representation but adds online refinement.

DAD~\cite{foster2021dad} uses two 64-unit tanh hidden layers, a trainable stage bias, and a sigmoid action map. Training uses pathwise gradients through the observations and carried states to maximize terminal sPCE. Before training, a random initialization is compared with an initialization based on the optimized Fixed sequence. The reported DAD preparation time includes this Fixed stage.

RL-sBOED~\cite{blau2022rlsboed} is implemented with a REDQ-style actor--critic using ten critics and five replay updates per trajectory batch. The actor has two 128-unit ReLU hidden layers. Its stage reward is $R_t=\widehat I_t-\widehat I_{t-1}$ with $\widehat I_0=0$, so the undiscounted rewards sum to terminal sPCE. Training uses entropy regularization with target entropy $-1$ in normalized action coordinates, and evaluation uses the deterministic actor output. Unlike DAD, RL-sBOED does not require pathwise differentiation through the swing simulator.

Step-DAD~\cite{hedman2025stepdad} starts from the selected DAD checkpoint. The first duration comes directly from the pretrained policy. After each subsequent measurement, a 128-particle posterior is used to simulate the remaining sequence, and the policy receives four Adam updates with batch size 32 and learning rate $10^{-3}$. A conditional validation batch determines whether the updated weights are retained. Accepted updates carry forward within the current probing sequence, while each new sequence starts from the original DAD checkpoint. Step-DAD is therefore adaptive at the policy level but retains substantial online computation.

\section{Experimental Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Simulation, Training, and Checkpoint Selection}
Experiments use $T=3,4,5$ probes, with one scalar RoCoF measurement per stage. A separate policy is trained for each network and horizon. The swing equations are integrated with fourth-order Runge--Kutta using a $1/640$-s time step. DAD and Step-DAD apply the chain rule through numerical Jacobians of the state and duration. State perturbations are $10^{-5}\max(1,|x_j|)$ and duration perturbations are $10^{-5}$ s, clipped to the feasible range. The resulting policy and refinement gradients are also checked against end-to-end finite differences.

Fixed, DAD, and RL-sBOED use training seeds 101, 202, and 303. Each run performs 2000 trajectory updates with batch size 32. Fixed and DAD use Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$; the RL actor and critics use $3\times10^{-4}$. Checkpoints are evaluated every 100 updates on 128 validation systems. Training, validation, and the initial evaluation use $L=128$ contrasts. The shared update count provides a common protocol, but it should not be interpreted as equal optimization effort or proof of convergence.

\subsection{Paired Evaluation and Statistical Reporting}\label{sec:evaluation}
Testing uses seeds 1001, 1002, and 1003, with 512 probing sequences per seed. Each fitted policy is therefore evaluated on 1536 sequences. Parameter and measurement-noise draws are paired across methods when the protocol allows; planner randomness is generated separately. Training, validation, and test seeds are distinct.

For Fixed and the adaptive policy-based methods, each reported value is the mean and sample standard deviation (SD) across three training-seed means, where each seed mean already averages all test sequences. Random and Myopic have no training seed, so their SDs are computed across the three test-seed means. The SDs therefore summarize seed-to-seed variation, not episode-level variability or uncertainty in paired method differences. We use these comparisons descriptively and do not make significance claims from the separate SDs.

\subsection{Offline Preparation and Online Decision Cost}\label{sec:cost}
Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports offline preparation and online decision time on NVIDIA A100 PCIe 40-GB systems using training seed 101 and test seed 1001. Online values are the mean total decision time over 512 test sequences. For IEEE 9-bus, one warm-up sequence per method is excluded. Offline values are single-run measurements and include initialization, optimization, and checkpoint validation. Because DAD initialization is compared against an optimized Fixed design, the DAD and Step-DAD preparation times include the Fixed stage.

The online timer includes action selection and, for Myopic and Step-DAD, posterior updating and refinement. It excludes propagation of the true test system, measurement acquisition, and terminal information scoring. Physical simulation runs on CUDA while policy and planning code also use the host, so the reported values are implementation-level decision times rather than pure neural-network inference times. Timing runs use the same $L=128$ scoring setting as the main experiments. Myopic uses 128 planning particles and 32 simulated observations per candidate duration. The lowest cost in each network--horizon column is shown in bold; ties are both bolded.


The main computational difference appears in the online decision times. DAD and RL-sBOED select durations with trained policies, whereas Myopic repeatedly simulates candidate probes. On IEEE 9-bus, DAD requires 1.012--1.745ms per sequence for $T=3$--$5$, compared with 4.418--7.514s for Myopic. DAD pays for this reduction with 1.116--2.139h of offline preparation, which is performed before the test sequences.

Step-DAD makes a different tradeoff. At IEEE 9-bus $T=5$, its mean score is 0.0269 nats above DAD, but online decision time rises from 1.745ms to 14.346s per sequence. At IEEE 14-bus $T=5$, Step-DAD requires 45.089s and still shows no consistent score improvement over DAD across horizons. Under this implementation, the small benefit of online refinement comes with a large decision-time cost. These are average implementation times, not worst-case latency guarantees.

For a dense $m$-machine model, one RK4 candidate simulation over $N$ time steps costs $C_s=O(Nm^2)$. If $C_\pi$ is the cost of one policy forward pass, online DAD and RL-sBOED require approximately $O(TC_\pi)$ policy \st{work}\hl{simulations?}. Myopic evaluates $Q=48$ candidates at each stage and repeatedly simulates posterior particles. Step-DAD additionally simulates future sequences during policy refinement. Offline Fixed and DAD costs grow with the number of updates, batch size, contrasts, and simulated stages, while RL-sBOED adds actor--critic replay updates instead of pathwise simulator derivatives. Increasing $L$ raises rescoring cost but does not change the already selected probes. \hl{if we are analyzing big O complexity of different methods, it might be good to summarize for the corresponding methods for clearer comparison?}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
The study uses reduced lossless models, fixed probe and sensor locations, the stated priors, and an imposed increasing-duration constraint. Equal probe counts do not imply equal injected energy. Training and testing also use the same prior, so prior mismatch, sensor bias, and unmodeled dynamics are outside the present evaluation. Myopic and Step-DAD use finite particles and limited online optimization and should not be interpreted as exact optima. Most importantly, sPCE is a lower bound on information about the joint parameter vector. It does not directly measure parameter-estimation error, frequency-security improvement, or closed-loop control performance. No explicit safety constraint is included, and the physical simulation assumes zero decision latency. The timing results therefore characterize algorithmic decision cost rather than complete real-time deployment feasibility.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper compared adaptive policy-based methods with Random, Fixed, and Myopic designs for sequential active probing with state carryover. On the reduced IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus systems, DAD, RL-sBOED, and Step-DAD achieve higher mean sPCE information scores across three to five probes. DAD and RL-sBOED perform most design optimization offline and keep online decision cost low relative to Myopic. Step-DAD provides small additional gains on IEEE 9-bus but requires much more online computation and gives no consistent improvement on IEEE 14-bus. Larger-contrast rescoring preserves the overall advantage of the adaptive policy-based methods while showing that close rankings remain estimator-sensitive. Future work should examine richer grid dynamics, model mismatch, practical sensing and latency, and safety-aware probing constraints.
