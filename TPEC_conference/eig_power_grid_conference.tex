\begin{abstract}

Reliable electricity supply for industry and households requires utilities and public authorities to manage uncertain grid dynamics, yet fixed probing cannot adapt to observations and myopic probing optimizes only the next experiment through repeated online computation. We study learned adaptive policies that select pulse durations from previous durations and noisy rate-of-change-of-frequency measurements to obtain more information about inertia and frequency response over a probing sequence. Deep adaptive design (DAD) and reinforcement-learning-based sequential design (RL-sBOED) train policies offline to amortize online design computation; Step-DAD adds online refinement to a pretrained DAD policy. We compare these methods with Random, Fixed, and Myopic on reduced IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus swing models. The learned adaptive methods achieve higher mean sequential prior-contrastive information scores than the baselines in both benchmarks. On IEEE 9-bus, DAD exceeds Fixed by 0.09--0.13 nats and requires 1.01--1.75 ms per sequence for online decisions on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Step-DAD provides modest additional gains on IEEE 9-bus at substantially greater online cost, with no consistent mean-score improvement on IEEE 14-bus. Rescoring IEEE 14-bus histories with more contrasts preserves the learned-policy advantage, although estimator sensitivity limits finer rankings.
Code and saved results are available at {\urlstyle{same}\url{https://github.com/alexschmidt123/tpec2027_power_grid}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Knowledge of grid dynamics supports planning and operation by utilities, system operators, and public energy agencies. The growing share of inverter-based generation changes inertia and frequency response~\cite{denholm2020inertia}. Characterizing these changes requires informative measurements. Disturbance-based approaches estimate inertia from frequency and active-power responses~\cite{wall2014inertia}. Active probing offers another source of measurements by deliberately exciting the system. Field tests show that probing-signal design affects electromechanical-mode estimates~\cite{pierre2010probing}, and recent work uses probing to estimate inertia and frequency response in systems with inverter-based resources~\cite{peng2024probing}. These studies motivate the choice of probing signals that reveal uncertain grid parameters.

An informative probe must be designed, and design computation can become a burden when decisions are repeated as measurements arrive. This is the central issue studied here. We consider a sequence of pulses with fixed amplitude and application bus, choosing each duration from the preceding durations and observations. The objective is to reduce joint uncertainty in inertia and frequency-response coefficients over the full sequence. Duration shapes the measured transient and injected energy, while the physical dynamics evolve between decisions. A useful design method must therefore be assessed both by the information it obtains and by the computation required to select the next probe. This extends the excitation-design question in system identification~\cite{hjalmarsson2009identification} to an adaptive sequential setting.

Bayesian optimal experimental design (BOED) measures the value of a probing sequence through expected information gain~\cite{huan2016sequential}. A fixed design optimizes the sequence in advance but cannot respond to new observations. A myopic design uses feedback, solving a one-step design problem online. Adaptive learning provides a different allocation of computation. Deep adaptive design (DAD)~\cite{foster2021dad} and reinforcement-learning-based sequential design (RL-sBOED)~\cite{blau2022rlsboed} train history-dependent policies offline for a full-sequence objective. During probing, a policy forward pass replaces repeated online design optimization. This amortization is the main computational motivation for applying these methods to grid probing. Step-DAD~\cite{hedman2025stepdad} retains the pretrained policy as a starting point and adds online refinement, allowing its information benefit to be weighed against the additional decision cost.

We evaluate DAD, RL-sBOED, and Step-DAD against Random, Fixed, and Myopic on reduced IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus models. The implementations share the same sensor, feasible duration constraints, probe counts, and sequential simulator. The study compares information scores, selected durations, offline preparation, and online decision times. Archived IEEE 14-bus histories are additionally rescored with more contrasts to check the dependence of method comparisons on the information estimator. The purpose is to determine where learned adaptive design obtains more information and where offline training reduces online computation. The comparison addresses the information and computational costs of adaptive probing as a step toward better grid characterization for planning and operation.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}
An episode consists of $T$ probes intended to reduce uncertainty in the grid parameters. We use $t=1,\ldots,T$ for the design stage, $d$ for physical time within a probe window, and $D_t$ for the pulse duration selected at that stage.

\subsection{Latent Parameters and Power-System Model}
For a reduced network with $m$ machines, the latent parameters are $\theta=(M_1,\ldots,M_m,K_1,\ldots,K_m)$. Here $M_i$ is an inertia coefficient and $K_i$ is an effective frequency-response coefficient. We assign independent uniform priors, $M_i\sim\mathcal U(0.7\bar M_i,1.3\bar M_i)$ and $K_i\sim\mathcal U(0.1\bar K_i,\bar K_i)$, denoted jointly by $p_0(\theta)$. The parameters are sampled once at the start of an episode and held fixed for all probes.

Let $\delta_i$ be rotor angle, $\omega_i$ angular-speed deviation, and $u_i$ the applied probing power. The reduced swing model is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot\delta_i &= \omega_i,\\
M_i\dot\omega_i
 &= u_i-\left(D_i+\frac{K_i}{2\pi}\right)\omega_i\\
 &\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{m}B_{ij}\sin(\delta_i-\delta_j).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:swing}
\end{equation}
Dots denote derivatives with respect to $d$. In~\eqref{eq:swing}, $M_i$ scales acceleration and $K_i$ models the power response to frequency deviation; no explicit turbine--governor dynamics are included. The damping $D_i$ and couplings $B_{ij}$ are known, and $\Delta f_i=\omega_i/(2\pi)$ is measured in Hz. On a 100-MVA base at 60 Hz, the units of $M_i$, $K_i$, and $D_i$ are pu$\,\mathrm{s}^2$/rad, pu/Hz, and pu$\,\mathrm{s}$/rad. Angles, speed deviations, and net mechanical injections initially equal zero, giving an unforced equilibrium.

The reduced IEEE 9-bus model retains buses 1, 2, and 3; the reduced IEEE 14-bus model retains buses 1, 2, 3, 6, and 8. Test-case reactances~\cite{zimmerman2011matpower} define a lossless Laplacian $\mathbf L$. Eliminating algebraic buses $l$ while retaining machines $g$ gives $\mathbf L_{\mathrm{red}}=\mathbf L_{gg}-\mathbf L_{gl}\mathbf L_{ll}^{-1}\mathbf L_{lg}$, with $B_{ij}=-(\mathbf L_{\mathrm{red}})_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$ and $B_{ii}=0$. Resistance, voltage dynamics, and transformer tap effects are omitted. Table~\ref{tab:physical} summarizes the model parameterization. Nominal inertia is computed as $\bar M_i=2H_i/(2\pi f_0)$, with $f_0=60$ Hz. For IEEE 9-bus, $H=(23.64,6.40,3.01)$ s matches published machine data~\cite[Table~2]{kaur2016transient}. For IEEE 14-bus, $H=(4,6.5,5,5,5)$ s is prescribed at buses $(1,2,3,6,8)$ as assumed benchmark values. For both networks, $\bar K_i=K_{\mathrm{tot}}w_i$, with $K_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.5$ pu/Hz. The allocation weights are $w=(0.5875,0.2625,0.15)$ for IEEE 9-bus and $w=(8,13,10,10,10)/51$ for IEEE 14-bus. Fixed damping satisfies $D_i=(0.1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1})\bar M_i$. The response scales, damping values, and prior ranges are benchmark assumptions rather than validated operational grid parameters.

\subsection{Probe Design and Observation Model}
A probe is specified by $\xi_t=(A_t,B_t,D_t)$, comprising its amplitude, application bus, and duration. We fix $A_t=0.05$ pu and $B_t=1$ so that duration is the only optimized variable. The applied input is a raised-cosine pulse:
\begin{equation}
u_{B_t,t}(d)
 =\frac{A_t}{2}\bigl[1-\cos(2\pi d/D_t)\bigr].
\label{eq:probe}
\end{equation}
The pulse acts over $0\le d\le D_t$ and is zero thereafter and at all other buses. Its injected energy is proportional to $A_tD_t/2$, so equal probe counts do not impose equal energy budgets. Each probe occupies a fixed observation window of $D_{\mathrm{obs}}=3.5$ s. The terminal angles and speeds become the initial state for the next window. Consequently, the response to probe $t$ depends on the preceding durations as well as the current duration, represented by $\xi_{1:t}=(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_t)$.

Let $\Delta f_{1,t}(d;\theta,\xi_{1:t})$ denote the bus-1 frequency deviation during window $t$. The noise-free scalar response $y_t$ is the signed endpoint rate of change of frequency (RoCoF):
\begin{equation}
y_t(\theta,\xi_{1:t})=
\frac{\Delta f_{1,t}(D_{\mathrm{obs}})-\Delta f_{1,t}(D_{\mathrm{obs}}-\Delta d_{\mathrm{samp}})}{\Delta d_{\mathrm{samp}}}.
\label{eq:sensor}
\end{equation}
The parameter and design arguments are suppressed in the numerator. With sampling interval $\Delta d_{\mathrm{samp}}=0.025$ s, the measured response follows
\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{obs},t}=y_t(\theta,\xi_{1:t})+\epsilon_t,
\qquad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
\label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma=0.005$ Hz/s and measurement noise is independent across windows. This sensor measures the post-pulse transient rather than the initial inertial response. Its fixed location and scalar output provide a common observation model for all methods. Observations become available at the end of each window; decision latency is idealized as zero in the simulated dynamics.

\subsection{Sequential Decisions and Bayesian Updating}
The data after step $t$ are the ordered design--observation history $h_t=((\xi_k,y_{\mathrm{obs},k}))_{k=1}^{t}$, with $h_0=\varnothing$. A policy $\pi$ selects $\xi_t$ using only observed history $h_{t-1}$, with amplitude and bus fixed as above. The policy has no access to the generating parameters or the true physical state. Feasible durations satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
0.2&\le D_t\le3, &&t=1,\ldots,T,\\
D_t-D_{t-1}&\ge \Delta D_{\min}, &&t=2,\ldots,T,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bounds}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta D_{\min}=0.01$ s. The lower bound is 0.2 s at the first stage and $D_{t-1}+\Delta D_{\min}$ thereafter; the upper bound is $3-(T-t)\Delta D_{\min}$, reserving room for the remaining probes. All methods map a unit action continuously into this interval, without sorting durations. Let $\Pi$ be the resulting policy class. Increasing durations are a benchmark restriction, not a requirement of the swing equations; nonmonotonic sequences are outside this comparison. Policy randomness is independent of $\theta$, and actions depend only on observed history.

For a candidate $\theta$, the predicted response and sensor noise define a Gaussian likelihood $p(y_{\mathrm{obs},t}\mid\theta,h_{t-1},\xi_t)$ with mean $y_t(\theta,\xi_{1:t})$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Incorporating the new measurement gives
\begin{equation}
p(\theta\mid h_t)=
\frac{p(y_{\mathrm{obs},t}\mid\theta,h_{t-1},\xi_t)\,p(\theta\mid h_{t-1})}
{p(y_{\mathrm{obs},t}\mid h_{t-1},\xi_t)}.
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
The denominator integrates over $\theta$; before the first measurement, $p(\theta\mid h_0)=p_0(\theta)$. Each candidate carries its own state through the observed duration sequence. No policy action probability enters the update: conditional on the observed history, actions depend on neither the unknown parameters nor their candidate states.



\subsection{Expected Information Gain and Design Objective}
The expected information gain (EIG) of policy $\pi$ measures the mean Kullback--Leibler divergence between the terminal posterior and the prior:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I_T(\pi)=\E_{h_T\mid\pi}
\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(\theta\mid h_T)\,\|\,p_0(\theta)\bigr)\right].
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
Here $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\int p(\theta)\log[p(\theta)/q(\theta)]\,d\theta$; information gain is measured in nats. The expectation averages over parameter draws, measurement noise, and any policy randomness. The sequential Bayesian design problem is
\begin{equation}
\pi^\star\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal I_T(\pi).
\label{eq:design_problem}
\end{equation}
All methods target~\eqref{eq:design_problem}, but their approximations and use of feedback differ. Probe counts are held equal, and computational costs are reported separately.

Evaluating EIG directly requires integration of the nonlinear likelihood over the uncertain parameters. The sequential prior-contrastive (sPCE) lower bound~\cite{foster2021dad} supplies a tractable alternative for training and evaluation. A generating draw $\theta^{(0)}\sim p_0$ produces the observed history, while $L$ independent draws $\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(L)}\sim p_0$ provide contrasts. Along the realized design sequence, a candidate's likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_t(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{t}
p(y_{\mathrm{obs},k}\mid\theta,h_{k-1},\xi_k).
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
The sampled information score is then
\begin{equation}
\widehat I_t=\log\frac{\mathcal L_t(\theta^{(0)})}
{\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\mathcal L_t(\theta^{(\ell)})}.
\label{eq:spce}
\end{equation}
We compute likelihoods in the log domain. The average of $\widehat I_T$ estimates the sPCE lower bound on $\mathcal I_T(\pi)$ rather than EIG itself. A sampled score can be negative and cannot exceed $\log(L+1)$. At $L=128$, this ceiling is 4.8598 nats and can obscure differences between informative designs. We therefore use the term information score for the estimated bound and examine contrast-count sensitivity separately. Generating parameters are used by the simulator and scorer, but are never supplied to the policy.

\section{Compared Design Methods}
All six methods use the same observation model and feasible duration
interval~\eqref{eq:bounds}. Adaptation and planning horizon are distinct: an adaptive method can be myopic, whereas a fixed sequence can be optimized jointly for the full horizon. The algorithms are adaptations of existing methods to the carried-state grid simulator.



\subsection{Nonadaptive Designs: Random and Fixed}
Random samples $z_t\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$ independently at each step and sets $D_t=D_t^{\min}+(D_t^{\max}-D_t^{\min})z_t$. Here $D_1^{\min}=0.2$, $D_t^{\min}=D_{t-1}+\Delta D_{\min}$ for $t>1$, and $D_t^{\max}=3-(T-t)\Delta D_{\min}$. Each duration is therefore uniform within the remaining feasible interval; complete sequences are not sampled uniformly. Random does not use observations and needs no offline training.

Fixed optimizes all durations before any test observations are collected~\cite[Sec.~2.4]{huan2016sequential}. Its objective is the full-sequence sPCE score. Validation first compares short, central, long, and spread interior initializations; optimization then proceeds from the selected sequence. A separate sequence is fitted for each training seed. At test time, these durations are executed without measurement feedback.

\subsection{Online One-Step Design: Myopic}
Myopic conditions each decision on the available measurements and targets information from the next observation~\cite[Sec.~2.4]{huan2016sequential}. A weighted particle approximation represents the current posterior. After a measurement, each particle advances its own physical state under the applied duration and receives an updated likelihood weight. The duration search uses three rounds of 16 proposals, with 32 simulated observations per proposal, and returns the best estimated one-step design. It respects the common duration constraints and requires no offline training.

\subsection{Offline-Trained Adaptive Methods: DAD and RL-sBOED}
DAD and RL-sBOED learn from sequences simulated under the parameter prior. Their inputs encode the stage index, past durations, scaled observations, and availability masks; future history slots are masked. Once trained, either policy selects a duration with a forward pass followed by the feasible-duration map. Explicit posterior assimilation and candidate-probe simulation are unnecessary during action selection.

The DAD implementation~\cite{foster2021dad} has two 64-unit tanh hidden layers, a trainable stage bias, and a sigmoid action map. Pathwise gradients propagate through observations and carried states to maximize the terminal sPCE score. Validation chooses between random initialization and initialization from a separately optimized Fixed sequence. The reported preparation cost includes the Fixed stage in either case (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

Our RL-sBOED implementation~\cite{blau2022rlsboed} uses a REDQ-style actor--critic algorithm with ten critics and five replay updates per trajectory batch. The actor has two 128-unit ReLU hidden layers. Rewards are $R_t=\widehat I_t-\widehat I_{t-1}$, with $\widehat I_0=0$ and discount factor $\gamma=1$, so their sum equals the terminal sPCE score. Training uses entropy regularization in normalized tanh-action coordinates with target entropy $-1$. Evaluation uses the deterministic actor output; reported information scores exclude the entropy bonus.

\subsection{Online Refinement: Step-DAD}
Step-DAD~\cite{hedman2025stepdad} begins each episode with the selected DAD checkpoint and retains its first action. After each nonterminal observation, a 128-particle posterior provides parameters and carried states for simulations of the remaining probes. A fresh Adam optimizer takes four updates before the next decision, using batch size 32 and learning rate $10^{-3}$. A common conditional validation batch compares the updated policies with the unchanged policy. The selected weights are retained within the episode and reset afterward. This gives $4(T-1)$ refinement updates. Both Myopic and Step-DAD assimilate observations before subsequent decisions; terminal information scoring is a separate operation.

\section{Experimental Settings}
\subsection{Simulation and Training Protocol}
We evaluate horizons $T=3,4,5$, with one scalar observation per probe. Policies are trained separately for each network and horizon; sensor settings and duration constraints are shared.

Simulation uses fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) integration with a $1/640$-s step. The pathwise gradient calculation approximates state and duration Jacobians numerically: perturbations are $10^{-5}\max(1,|x_j|)$ for state coordinate $x_j$ and $10^{-5}$ s for duration, with duration perturbations clipped at the feasible bounds. Policy and conditional-refinement gradients are checked against end-to-end finite differences in the validation suite.

Each network--horizon combination has Fixed, DAD, and RL-sBOED fits from training seeds 101, 202, and 303. Training comprises 2000 trajectory updates of batch size 32. Fixed and DAD use Adam at $10^{-3}$; the RL actor and critics use $3\times10^{-4}$. Every 100 updates, 128 validation systems determine checkpoint selection. Training, validation, and initial evaluation use $L=128$ contrasts. The larger-$L$ IEEE 14-bus evaluation is described in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}. Myopic and Step-DAD each use 128 planning particles, and Step-DAD takes four refinement updates after each nonterminal observation.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evaluation}
Test seeds 1001, 1002, and 1003 supply 512 episodes each, or 1536 episodes per fitted policy. Methods share paired parameter and measurement-noise draws; planner randomness uses a separate stream. Training, checkpoint validation, and testing also use separate seeds. For fitted methods, tables report the mean and sample standard deviation (SD) of three training-seed means, each averaged over all test episodes. Random and Myopic do not depend on a training seed, so their SDs are taken over the three test-seed means. These SDs quantify variation among seed means. They neither describe episode dispersion nor provide uncertainty intervals for paired method differences.

\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{sec:cost}
Table~\ref{tab:grid_a100} reports timings on NVIDIA A100 PCIe 40-GB GPUs, using training seed 101 and test seed 1001. Offline preparation includes initialization, optimization, and checkpoint validation; DAD and Step-DAD include both Fixed and DAD training. Online time is the mean total decision time per sequence over 512 test episodes per method. It includes action selection and, where applicable, posterior assimilation and refinement; true-system propagation and information scoring are excluded. IEEE 9-bus profiling excludes one warm-up episode per method. Offline times are single-run measurements. For all six methods (Random, Fixed, Myopic, DAD, RL-sBOED, and Step-DAD), the timed runs use the same contrast count, $L=128$, for information scoring. Training, validation, and Step-DAD refinement also use $L=128$; Myopic uses 128 posterior particles and 32 simulated observations per candidate. Increasing $L$ increases evaluation scoring time, which is excluded from the decision timers.

Let $C_s$ denote one candidate-window simulation cost and $C_\pi$ a policy forward pass. Dense $m$-machine RK4 integration over $N$ time steps gives $C_s=O(Nm^2)$.

Online policy work is $O(T)$ for Random/Fixed and $O(TC_\pi)$ for DAD/RL-sBOED. Myopic uses $O(TQC_e+TP C_s)$, where $P=128$ particles, $Q=48$ candidate evaluations per decision, and $C_e$ includes candidate simulation and likelihood calculations. Step-DAD refinement simulates $UB(L+1)T(T-1)/2$ candidate windows before accounting for numerical Jacobians and conditional validation, with $U=4$, $B=32$, and $L=128$. Posterior assimilation adds $O(TP C_s)$ work.

We rescore all 64\,512 distinct IEEE 14-bus histories at $L=10^4$ and $10^5$, holding policies, generating parameters, durations, and observations fixed. No policies are retrained or rerun. Contrasts are drawn from the archived prior; matching test-seed/episode pairs share the contrast stream across methods, and the $10^4$ samples are a prefix of the $10^5$ samples. Archived terminal states and original scores are reproduced before rescoring. Tables~\ref{tab:ieee14_largeL} and~\ref{tab:ieee14_largeL_100000} report mean $\pm$ SD using the seed aggregation defined in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}; seed-level summaries are included in the repository.

Myopic exceeds Fixed at every horizon, reversing their $L=128$ ordering for $T=4,5$. That reversal demonstrates finite-contrast sensitivity; it does not identify ceiling saturation as the sole cause. At $L=10^5$, Step-DAD differs from DAD by $-0.0062$, $-0.0082$, and $+0.0016$ nats for $T=3,4,5$, so refinement has no consistent mean benefit on IEEE 14-bus. DAD's $T=5$ mean rises from 8.666 to 9.693 nats between the two larger contrast counts. The additional contrasts alleviate the original ceiling effect, but the residual change prevents a claim of convergence or a definitive EIG ranking among learned policies.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
These results concern reduced lossless dynamics, specified parameter priors, increasing pulse durations, and a single fixed actuator and endpoint sensor. The sPCE score estimates a lower bound on expected joint uncertainty reduction; individual parameter-estimation accuracy is not evaluated. Training and testing use the same prior, leaving prior shift, sensor bias, and unmodeled dynamics outside the present assessment. Finite contrasts, posterior particles, and numerical integration introduce separate approximations. Finally, zero decision latency is assumed in the simulated dynamics, so the measured algorithm costs alone do not establish real-time feasibility or improved control safety.

\section{Conclusion}
We examined adaptive learning for obtaining more information from sequential grid probing while moving design computation offline. DAD, RL-sBOED, and Step-DAD were compared with Random, Fixed, and Myopic on reduced IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus models with uncertain inertia and effective frequency response. Learned adaptive policies obtain higher mean sPCE scores than the tested baselines at equal probe counts. DAD and RL-sBOED place optimization offline, while Step-DAD spends additional online computation for modest IEEE 9-bus gains. On IEEE 14-bus, larger-$L$ evaluation retains the learned-policy advantage, reverses the longer-horizon Fixed--Myopic ordering, and shows no consistent refinement benefit. Residual estimator sensitivity limits finer EIG comparisons. The results support further investigation of sequential probing under model mismatch and practical sensing and latency constraints.
